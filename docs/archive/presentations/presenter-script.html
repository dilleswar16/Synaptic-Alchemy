Topic 01 — OTT Thumbnails
3 slides: Hook · Myth · Sprite Sheet · CDN
Hook slide
"Quick question — when you're scrubbing through a Netflix show, dragging the seek bar left and right really fast, you see a preview thumbnail that appears instantly. No spinner, no delay. How?" pause — let them guess
"Is Netflix secretly playing the video in the background? Is there an AI analysing frames in real time? Is it firing a network request on every pixel you drag?" click →
Myth slide
"All of those sound reasonable. All of them are wrong."
"Decoding video is expensive — even a 2-second clip takes hundreds of milliseconds. Drag events fire 60 times per second — no API can keep up. And background streaming would kill your battery instantly." click →
Sprite Sheet slide
"This is the trick. It's called a sprite sheet. When a video is uploaded, a background job grabs one frame every 10 seconds. A two-hour movie — that's roughly 720 frames."
"All 720 frames are stitched side by side into one single image. Like a film strip. Downloaded once. Cached in your browser."
"When you drag to 34% of the video, the browser does: 34% of 720 = frame 245. Row = 245 ÷ 10 = row 24. Column = 245 mod 10 = column 5. Then it shifts the CSS background position by a few pixels. No network call. No decoding. Under a millisecond."
"That seek bar is just doing division. Zero AI involved." click →
💡 Bonus point: By the way, when I say 'instant play', I mean the current video starts instantly — not the next episode. Netflix has already downloaded the first 30 seconds to the server near you before you even opened the app. That's why pressing play = video plays immediately.
CDN slide
"Now why is it fast globally? The CDN — Content Delivery Network. Without one, your request goes India → US data centre → back. That's 200ms minimum, just from the physics of distance."
"With a CDN, Netflix has 15,000+ edge servers worldwide — including in your city. Round trip is 4ms."
"But here's the real trick — Netflix doesn't wait for you to press play. They pre-push popular content to edge servers before you even open the app. Think Amazon warehouses — stock is near you before you order. Netflix does the same with content."
"'Instant play' is just revealing something that was already sitting near you."
"And by the way — Netflix offline downloads? Same DRM encryption. The file is on your phone but locked to the app. Your gallery can't open it. Your phone has the bytes but not a usable video file. That's why the screen goes black when you try to screen-record Netflix — it detects the capture API and blocks it. Pure copyright protection." click →
💡 Bonus points if asked:
① What if content isn't cached? If a niche indie doc isn't pre-pushed to your region, the request falls back to the origin server — maybe 200-500ms instead of 4ms. Still faster than no CDN, but noticeably slower.
② How does Netflix decide what to pre-cache in India? They analyze regional viewing patterns. In India, they pre-cache Bollywood and Hindi originals more aggressively than anime — because data shows Indians watch more Bollywood.
③ Why do you see a BLACK SCREEN when recording Netflix? That's DRM (Digital Rights Management) — Widevine protection. Not CDN-related. Netflix detects screen recording and goes black to prevent piracy. Same on Disney+, Amazon Prime, Spotify. It's copyright protection, not a delivery problem.

Topic 02 — Food Delivery Tracking
3 slides: Hook · GPS · Interpolation
Topic transition
"Alright, OTT done. Moving to something you probably checked while waiting for this session to start — your food delivery." light pause
Hook slide
"You order on Swiggy or Zomato. Within seconds there's a little motorbike icon on the map. And it moves. Smoothly. Like it's gliding. How?" pause — click →
GPS slide
"Your phone's GPS updates location every 1 to 4 seconds. And it's not perfectly accurate — there's noise, signal bounce, a few metres of margin."
"If you plotted raw GPS dots on a map, the rider would look like they're jumping around randomly. Not smooth at all." click →
Interpolation slide
"This is where interpolation comes in. The app doesn't show you where the rider is — it shows you where it thinks the rider is between GPS pings."
"GPS ping — rider at point A. 3 seconds later — rider at point B. In between, the app animates the icon smoothly from A to B at a calculated speed. Linear interpolation."
"On top of that, map-matching snaps the GPS coordinates to the actual road. If GPS says the rider is 8m to the left of the road — physically impossible — the algorithm corrects it to the road."
"What looks like a live feed is a prediction engine running at 60 frames per second, constantly filling in the gaps." click →
💡 Deep dive (if asked about backend): So how does the rider's GPS actually get to your phone? It's not a direct connection.
Here's the real flow: Rider's GPS → sent to backend API every 2-5 seconds → backend pushes to Kafka (a message queue, think middleman) → Kafka broadcasts to all customers watching that rider → your phone receives via WebSocket (not polling).
Why not direct? One rider might have 50 customers watching. Kafka handles all that broadcasting without the rider's phone breaking. Plus if you disconnect and reconnect, Kafka has the last few updates queued so you catch up instantly.
The smoothness you see? That's your browser doing interpolation after receiving each WebSocket update. Raw GPS (noisy) → backend → Kafka → WebSocket → your phone smooths it → animated dot.
💡 Bonus connection (if someone asks about other apps): By the way, this exact pattern — one WebSocket connection with multiple topic subscriptions — is used in every real-time app. Groww and Zerodha use it for stock prices. One connection to the backend, subscribe to INFY + TCS + RELIANCE + your portfolio, and all updates come through the same pipe. Gaming apps use it for multiplayer positions. Discord uses it for messages. The principle is the same everywhere: efficient, scalable, real-time.

Topic 03 — Google Maps Traffic
3 slides: Hook · Sensors · Prediction
Topic transition
"Okay, food delivery covered. Now — how many of you opened Google Maps today? Even once?" wait for hands
"The moment you opened it, you became a data point in Google's traffic system. Not when your phone is idle — only when Maps is running. But think about rush hour: millions of commuters have it open at the same time. That is the crowd. And that's how Google knows about your jam before you're even in it." click →
Hook slide
"Google Maps shows you real-time traffic — red, orange, green. How does it know? Are there cameras on every road? Government sensors?" pause — click →
Sensor slide
"Government sensors exist on some highways. They cover maybe 1% of roads globally. That's not what Maps is using."
"The real source is you. Every phone running Maps continuously sends anonymised location data — speed, heading, position. Millions of data points per minute."
"If 50 phones on a stretch of road all report 8 km/h, Google knows there's a jam. No camera needed. They have a crowd." click →
Prediction slide
"Real-time is only half of it. Maps blends current speed with years of historical data using a weighted average — every road, every hour, every day of the week."
"If there's a cricket match ending at 6pm nearby, Maps has seen what happens after every IPL match at that stadium for 5 years. It factors that in."
"When it says 'leave now — 32 minutes, leave in 10 — 45 minutes', that's a machine learning model trained on billions of historical trips." click →
💡 Bonus point (if asked about data accuracy or manipulation): You might think: 'What if people are walking with phones? Won't that show as traffic?' or 'What if 50 people on a bus skew the data?'
Google has filters. Pedestrians move 3-5 km/h — Google filters out anything below ~10 km/h on roads. Walking doesn't count as traffic.
And if 50 phones cluster together moving at the same speed? Google treats them as one data point (assumes they're in the same vehicle).
Plus map-matching snaps coordinates to actual road segments — data off-road is excluded.
Bottom line: Google has billions of data points + ML filtering. A few anomalies don't move the needle. The aggregate pattern is too strong.

Topic 04 — Shazam
3 slides: Hook · Constellation · Fingerprint
Topic transition
"Maps done. Now this next one — this is probably the most magical-feeling app on your phone. And the engineering behind it is almost unfairly clever." pause
Hook slide
"Noisy bar. Music playing. Someone's shouting next to you. You hold your phone up for 5 seconds and Shazam tells you the exact song, artist, album. How?" pause — click →
Constellation slide — spectrogram image
"Shazam converts the audio into a spectrogram — what you're looking at on screen. Time on the X axis. Frequency on the Y axis. Brightness = loudness."
"Every song has a unique fingerprint in a spectrogram. The melody creates peaks at specific frequencies at specific times — like stars in a constellation."
"Background noise IS in the spectrogram. But noise is random — scattered dots everywhere. The song's peaks are consistent and structured. Shazam finds the loudest peaks and ignores the noise." click →
Fingerprint slide — violin spectrogram
"See those bright lines on screen? Those are harmonics — the note plus its overtones. Completely consistent. Every time you play that clip, those lines appear in exactly the same place."
"Shazam doesn't send the full audio to the server. It takes pairs of peaks — frequency A, frequency B, time gap — and hashes them into a number. Your 5-second clip generates about 20 hash pairs. That's less than 1 kilobyte."
"The server has pre-indexed 11 million songs — same algorithm, same fingerprints. It looks up your 20 hashes and finds the match in under a second."
"Like identifying a face not from a full photo — just the distance between the eyes and the shape of the nose. A few measurements. Unique enough."
"Oh — and one more thing. Shazam was built in 2002. Before smartphones existed. The original product? You called a phone number, held your phone to the speaker, and got a text message back with the song name. In 2002. On a feature phone. The algorithm has been running for over 20 years." let that land — click →
💡 Bonus points (word-for-word if asked):
"If you ask why Shazam is fast, here's the punchline: it uploads under 1 KB, not a full 30 MB song file."
"If you ask about covers and remixes: Shazam usually identifies the exact version, because the peak pattern changes between studio, live, remix, and acoustic versions."
"If you ask why it's called a constellation map: those bright spectral peaks look like stars in a sky, and each song has a unique star pattern."
"If you ask about noisy places: random crowd noise does not form stable peak pairs, so the algorithm discards it and keeps only strong repeated peaks."

Topic 05 — OTP, Cards & CVV
4 slides: Hook · TOTP · Luhn · CVV
Topic transition
"Alright, Shazam done. Now we get into the one that touches money — always makes people pay attention." pause
Hook slide
"You open your Authenticator app. Airplane mode on. No internet. It still shows the correct 6-digit OTP. And your bank accepts it. How are both sides generating the same number with zero communication?" pause — click →
TOTP slide
"It's called TOTP — Time-based One-Time Password. When you scanned that QR code, your bank sent your phone one thing — a shared secret key. Transferred once. Stored securely."
"Every 30 seconds, both your phone and the bank's server independently run: shared secret + current timestamp rounded to 30 seconds → HMAC-SHA1 hash → take 6 digits."
"Same secret, same time — same 6 digits. No communication needed. Expires every 30 seconds because the timestamp changes."
"And if you're wondering — even in airplane mode, how does the server know which 30-second window your OTP came from? It doesn't need to ask. Both clocks run independently. Server checks current window, previous window, and next window to handle minor clock drift. No sync needed. Just math." click →
Luhn slide
"Your card number also has math built in. The last digit is a Luhn check digit. Double every second digit from the right. If it exceeds 9, subtract 9. Sum everything. If divisible by 10 — valid card."
"This is why when you mistype your card number online, the form catches it before any server is called. Pure math, client-side. Invented in 1954." click →
CVV slide
"The CVV — 3 digits on the back. Not random. It's computed from your card number + expiry date + a secret bank key using DES encryption."
"The bank never stores your CVV. When you enter it online, they recompute it and compare. No database lookup. If it matches — the card is genuine." click →
Chip vs Magnetic Stripe slide
"Now this is the core difference between old magnetic-stripe cards and modern EMV chip cards."
"On magnetic stripe, card data is static — same track data every swipe. So if a skimmer — that's a hidden device criminals attach to ATMs or POS machines to read your card — captures that data once, they can clone your card and replay transactions."
"On EMV chip, each transaction creates a fresh one-time cryptogram. Even if someone captures one transaction, they cannot reuse it for the next payment."
"Simple summary: magnetic stripe is reusable data, EMV is one-time proof. That is why chip and tap are much safer than swipe." click →
OTP/Cards bonus Q&A lines
💡 Bonus points (word-for-word if asked):
"If you ask how OTP works without internet: both your phone and the bank already share the same secret key, and both run the same math every 30 seconds. Same secret + same time = same code."
"If you ask why OTP expires: the timestamp window changes every 30 seconds, so old codes instantly become invalid."
"If you ask whether CVV is stored: banks do not store CVV for normal payment processing. They recompute and verify in real time."
"If you ask magnetic stripe vs chip: magnetic stripe is static data, so if copied once, it can be replayed. EMV chip creates a fresh cryptogram for every transaction, so copied data cannot be reused."
"If you ask about tap payments: tap is still EMV security over NFC. The transport is contactless, but the cryptography is the same dynamic EMV model."
"If you ask about safety: OTP proves you currently hold the second factor, but phishing can still steal it, so app-based authenticators are safer than SMS OTP."

Topic 06 — GPS & Einstein
3 slides: Hook · How GPS works · Einstein
Topic transition
"Last topic. And I saved this one for last because it has the biggest 'wait, what?' moment of the whole session. This one involves physics. And a 100-year-old dead scientist. Let me explain." pause
Hook slide
"24 satellites orbiting at 20,000km altitude. Your phone figures out its position to within 3 metres using just radio waves and math. No internet. No ground stations." pause — click →
How GPS works — animated constellation GIF
"Watch the animation on screen — 24 satellites always arranged so at least 4 are visible from any point on Earth at any time."
"Each satellite broadcasts: 'I am Satellite 7. My position is X, Y, Z. The time is 14:23:00.000000000.' That's it. A simple radio broadcast."
"Your phone receives 4 signals at slightly different times — because each satellite is a different distance away. Radio waves travel at the speed of light — 30 centimetres per nanosecond. So a 1-microsecond delay = 300 metres difference."
"4 distance measurements → 4 spheres → one unique point where all 4 overlap. That's your location. It's called trilateration — not triangulation, people always say triangulation!" click →
Einstein slide
"Without Einstein, your GPS would drift by 11 kilometres per day."
"Satellites move at 14,000 km/h. Special Relativity — moving clocks run slower. Satellite clocks lose 7 microseconds per day."
"But satellites are further from Earth's gravity. General Relativity — clocks in weaker gravity run faster. Satellite clocks gain 45 microseconds per day."
"Net effect: plus 38 microseconds per day. At the speed of light, 38 microseconds = 11 kilometres of drift."
"So the satellites run their clocks slightly slow on purpose to compensate. Einstein published his theory in 1905. GPS engineers implemented it in 1970s hardware."
"Every time you drop a pin in Google Maps, you're using Einstein's theory of relativity. Casually." click →
💡 Bonus points (word-for-word if asked):
"If you ask why GPS needs 4 satellites: three solve position, and the fourth corrects your phone clock error."
"If you ask GPS vs internet: GPS gives location from satellites; internet only helps download maps and traffic layers faster."
"If you ask why GPS fails near tall buildings: signals reflect off glass and concrete, creating multipath errors."
"If you ask why I mention Einstein: because the correction is not theory only, it's literally hardcoded into satellite timing systems."
"If you ask about India's own GPS: India has NavIC — Navigation with Indian Constellation — 7 satellites covering India and 1,500km around it. Used in modern Android phones and Indian defence systems. We're not just using American GPS anymore."
